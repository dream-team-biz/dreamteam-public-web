How a brainstorm turned a problem into nine tasks

The problem it solves

Tasks run out of order and stop halfway(tidied)

A real example: BS-117, from its own records, and where the engineer changed its course. Its numbers are in teal. The method →

Lab note · DreamTeam Lab · October 2026

The problem

Two tasks listed as ready at once; their order lived only in a note

DreamTeam traced his case to two tasks the board listed as ready at once: a safety guard for unattended runs, and a board-report command. Both rewrite the same two generated files. The guard task also needed a third task's output.

A fourth had stopped before any code, assuming two mechanisms no task had built.

In BS-117 · the vision

the board lists both as ready to dispatch simultaneously.

The vision (its goal and requirements) on the two tasks, word for word.

In tasks.yaml · T-693's notes

… Lands before T-687 when both are ready (shared generated files). …
depends_on: []

T-693's record on 18 September, word for word.

S1 · Vision

Twelve questions, and one answer that turned the job

A brainstorm is an interview that turns an idea into a plan. DreamTeam set up and researched before it asked: two agents searched the repository; it ran six web searches itself.

Then it interviewed the engineer in three rounds, until its own confidence estimate reached 0.80; it stated 0.86.

In BS-117 · the session, S1

“Your diagnosis is right, and the mechanism is weaker than you think.”

How DreamTeam's findings began, after its research, word for word.

Where he changed course. His one typed answer said the check that stops a task was fine and the links were never written, so the vision narrowed to writing them down. Two more answers went against its recommendation.

S2 · Design

His question took the authoring rule to every place a task is born

The design came in ten numbered parts. Then the engineer asked: Does it cover tasks born outside a brainstorm, such as follow-ups and splits?(tidied) DreamTeam answered: “Good catch, and the honest answer is: partly.”

The design already refused, in code, to save a task without its links, in the one command that creates task rows. But the authoring rule, an instruction wherever a task is written, covered only the brainstorm's plan.

It widened that rule to all five places a task is born, and he took the low-level design it offered, choosing its recommended option.

S3 · Draft plan

Nine tasks in waves, each link saying why

The plan came out as eight tasks and one that closes the epic, the larger goal they serve. A wave is the tasks that can run together, worked out from the links; a collision never orders.

It flagged its own size: one task over the five to seven the engineer had set. He kept them all, the option it recommended. The drawing shows the plan after his later ruling of 19 September, marked ◆.

In tasks.yaml · T-701's link to T-700

- on: T-700
  kind: build-order
  why: waits for T-700 to close (…); both edit `config/workflows.yaml` and regenerate the same commands, and T-700 leads because it is already in flight

His ruling of 19 September, as the board records it, word for word, the role cut with “…”.

S4 · Panel

Three reviewers and the orchestrator: 39 findings, a decision on each

Three reviewer agents, run separately and told not to edit, read the plan; none edited a file, and all asked for changes. The orchestrator, the agent that runs the session, put four items to the engineer.

The finding that mattered most was about the design itself. The registry check is a CI job the repository's ruleset makes required: a pull request that fails it cannot merge without an admin's bypass.

What it saved · an assumption

  • Had the new rule been on before the backfill, every pull request would have failed it, whatever it changed: the check reads the whole board. About 54 pull requests over some 40 hours were exposed.
  • The repository's own precedent: a check of the same kind “actually broke on master” when it was switched on ahead of its rows.
  • R2: 24 tests broke at the narrower refusal R2 asked for, as predicted; the broader one, never run, would have hit about 125 of 156 fixtures, by the orchestrator's estimate.

In T-701 · its runlog

AC-1 originally named dead code, AC-3 originally named an already-allocated invariant number

Its closure notes, an excerpt, word for word. Its lesson: check a contract against the live code when the task starts, “not just at plan time”.

What the panel did not catch. T-701, the fourth planned task, found both errors before its work began, 19 September, 20:27–20:39 UTC. The design named a function nothing called, and the reviewer took it for the live one; no reviewer was asked to check callers. The invariant number was free at review and taken two days later.

S5–S6 · Plan and materialize

One approval, then nine tasks on the board

The compiled plan lists every change by the finding behind it. He approved it whole, with the option “Approve all”. After S1, every answer matched an offered option; wherever one was recommended, he took it.

Then S6 materialized the plan, writing it onto the board: a script wrote the nine tasks, their criteria copied word for word from the approved plan.

After the brainstorm

Nine became fourteen, and the engineer closed the epic

One task split in two and four were added, all five born outside a brainstorm, on paths the authoring rule covered only after his S2 question. The plan against reality, and its cost, are in the Lab note Anatomy of one epic.

The small print

Sources and method
  • Sources. The record cards quote DreamTeam's repository at 6b947dfb1, except T-693's notes (feeceef05fa4, the board on 18 September) and DreamTeam's opening line, from the recorded session, outside Git. So are the interview, the reviewers' reports, the breaks and the timing; each count was redone from it.
  • Question 1 offered four options: “Dispatch + CI, both hard” (recommended), “Dispatch only, hard block”, “CI only, hard block”, “Warn everywhere, never block”. He typed his own answer.
  • “Chosen” means the answer matches an offered option word for word; the record cannot show a click from typing.
  • The estimate counts an area the interview must cover only when his answer is quoted; the 0.86 left one, which files change, partial.
  • The waves were recomputed at S4 by the architect; every row matched.
  • The four items went to the engineer because they would change an approved part of the design or, in the panel file's words, “a ruling obtained under a premise that turned out to be false”.
  • The clocks. The day's parts are the gaps between a question and its answer, split by whether the session was closed in between; DreamTeam's time is the rest. The log includes the engineer's S1 answers and leaves out about 38 minutes of DreamTeam's work, among them the low-level design and compiling the plan.
  • No written link was broken: none pointed at a missing task, and no finished task waited on an open one; a check for loops was not built yet.
  • Earlier cases, no rate. T-611 and T-614 edited the same file; the collision was flagged but never recorded as one. T-556's link to T-566 was left out on purpose, accepting a red CI run, the engineer's own choice. The record gives no rate.
  • The wrong statement. The stage files say his first “Include it” was given under that statement; the session shows that answer about five minutes before the statement was first written.
  • The rework estimate counts the merges from #1179 to #1235, between T-701's merge and the backfill's last batch, about 40 hours 35 minutes; derived, not measured.
  • Tidied. The engineer's three passages are tidied: spelling and grammar corrected, the problem and his question shortened, the meaning kept.
