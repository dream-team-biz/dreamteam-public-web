Why DreamTeam?

AI can write the code. Someone still has to understand it and answer for it. That isn’t a tool you install — it’s a process you build. DreamTeam runs the whole development cycle with AI agents, and engineers own every result.

01 · The shift

Software rested on one assumption for fifty years. AI removed it.

The assumption: the person who wrote the code understands it and answers for it. Planning, review, testing and release were built on it.

Now AI writes much of the code, faster than anyone can read it. In February 2025 Andrej Karpathy named it “vibe coding”, Collins’ Word of the Year by November. A year later he suggested a name for the disciplined version: agentic engineering.

What it means: The tools got faster at writing code. The process that keeps engineers in control of it has not caught up.

02 · What the industry has learned

Five lessons the industry has learned, and one open question

  1. Speed is not delivery

    The same report found that more AI adoption goes with higher delivery throughput, and still with lower delivery stability.

    25–35% of the time from idea to launch is writing and testing code: Bain & Company, Technology Report 2025, from surveys of SaaS developer teams

    What it means: AI speeds up a small part of the work and amplifies the process around it.

  2. Review is the new bottleneck

    Agreeing to buy Graphite, a code-review company, in December 2025, Cursor wrote: “As writing code has become faster, reviewing changes, merging them safely, and collaborating effectively have increasingly become the bottlenecks …”

    +23% pull requests merged on GitHub, year on year; comments on issues and pull requests flat (+0.35%)

    What it means: more changes merge with about the same discussion, so each gets less.

  3. You can’t own what you can’t explain

    The Linux kernel made it policy: the human submitter is responsible for “reviewing all AI-generated code” and “taking full responsibility for the contribution”.

    50% vs 67% quiz scores after learning a new library with AI or by hand: 52 mostly junior engineers, randomized

    What it means: people who delegate the writing understand less of it, so ownership has to be built into the process.

  4. Code nobody understands is a debt

    Storey calls it cognitive debt; Addy Osmani, an engineering leader now at Anthropic, comprehension debt. Both are argued more than measured; the code has been measured:

    +30% static-analysis warnings in 806 open-source repositories after adopting Cursor; the speed-up faded, the warnings stayed

    What it means: code nobody can explain is paid for later, by whoever has to change it.

  5. Agents need written requirements, and a checker who is not the builder

    SWE-bench Verified is a set of real GitHub issues with tests that grade a fix; METR’s agents had one attempt and no feedback. On SWE-Bench Pro, whose “unit tests expect a narrow set of solutions”, fewer fixes passed once the written requirements were removed:

    25.9% → 8.4% tasks passed by the stronger of two models on SWE-Bench Pro, with and without written requirements and interface

    What it means: without written requirements, neither the builder nor the checker knows what done means.

  6. The open question: lights off, or lights on

    StrongDM does check, without people: against scenarios kept outside the codebase, because “a test, stored in the codebase, can be lazily rewritten to match the code.” Addy Osmani’s rule for where the lights stay on: a loop “needs to be reviewed if a wrong answer is expensive and only a person can catch it.”

    What it means: the choice is about process, not tools.

03 · Process, not tools

Better models write better code. They don’t decide what to build.

What to build, what done means and who answers for the result stay with people: the kernel’s rule is about responsibility, not capability.

What it means: The gap is not in the tools. It is in the process around them.

04 · What we build

DreamTeam is a team of AI agents doing a software team’s work — architect, developers, reviewer, tester, technical writer — with engineers in control.

The small print

Numbers you may have seen, and what their sources say
  • “AI code costs 4× more to maintain by year two.” We found no study behind it; it travels through unlinked blog posts. Measured instead: the lasting rise in static-analysis warnings of lesson 4 (He et al., Carnegie Mellon, MSR 2026).
  • “76% ship AI code they don’t understand.” Qodo, an AI code-review company, surveyed 609 developers in 2025 and placed 76% in its “red zone” of “frequent hallucinations” and “low confidence in AI-generated code” — developers its report says “avoid shipping AI-generated code without human checks” (Qodo, 2025 State of AI code quality). It does not measure understanding. Measured instead: 66% find AI answers “almost right, but not quite” (Stack Overflow survey, 2025), and the quiz in lesson 3.
  • “90% of developers use AI, but only 13% across the whole lifecycle.” Two figures from two sources. The 90% is from DORA 2025: “90% of survey respondents report using AI at work” (Google Cloud, September 2025). The 13% is a best-case ceiling on cycle time, not a share of teams: if coding is 40% of cycle time, 60% of that coding can be supported by an assistant, and the team is 55% faster with it, cycle time falls by at most 13%, “for this scenario with very optimistic assumptions” (Sichu Zhang, Thoughtworks, February 2025).
  • “Review time is up 91%.” Faros AI’s telemetry of over 10,000 developers on 1,255 teams compared each team’s two lowest- and two highest-AI-adoption quarters: review time rose 91%, and it “observed no significant correlation between AI adoption and improvements at the company level” (The AI Productivity Paradox Report, July 2025). Vendor-reported and correlational; the independent figure is lesson 2’s.
  • “8× more duplicated code.” GitClear’s 2025 report, on one narrow measure (vendor-reported); its multiplier differs from telling to telling. Independent: duplicated lines up 7%, not significant, in the 806 repositories of lesson 4 (He et al.).
  • “55% faster with AI.” One task, an HTTP server in JavaScript: freelancers finished in 71 minutes on average with GitHub Copilot, against 161 without (95 recruited; Peng et al., 2023). Across three field experiments with 4,867 developers: 26% more completed tasks (Cui et al., 2025).
  • “19% slower with AI.” 16 experienced developers in their own repositories, with early-2025 tools; they believed they had been 20% faster (METR, 2025). METR’s follow-up could not produce a reliable estimate and says developers are probably faster now (METR, February 2026).
  • “95% of AI pilots fail.” As an 80,000 Hours critique (April 2026) reads a preliminary report by MIT’s Project NANDA (July 2025; 52 interviews, 153 survey responses): the 95% counts every organization without a custom AI tool in production showing “marked and sustained” impact within six months, including the 80% that never piloted one and pilots that broke even. It is not a pilot-failure rate; we found none for software teams.
Sources and method

Checked on 13 October 2026. Every quote was read at its source — the person’s own post, the interview, the report, the policy page — and is given word for word; “…” marks words left out. Each number comes with who was measured, how many, and when.

The shift

  • “Vibe coding”: Andrej Karpathy’s post on X, 2 February 2025: “There’s a new kind of coding I call “vibe coding”, where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials …” Collins: “Coined by AI pioneer Andrej Karpathy, vibe coding refers to the use of artificial intelligence prompted by natural language …”, Word of the Year 2025 (Collins Language Blog, 6 November 2025).
  • “Agentic engineering”: Andrej Karpathy’s post on X, 4 February 2026, a year after “vibe coding”: his “current favorite” name; “agentic” for the reason on the card, “engineering” “to emphasize that there is an art & science and expertise …”. His site calls him “an AI researcher and educator”.
  • Addy Osmani, named in lessons 4 and 6, defined it that week: “Agentic engineering = AI does the implementation, human owns the architecture, quality, and correctness.” (Agentic Engineering, 4 February 2026). His site describes him as “an Irish engineering and evangelism leader and a Member of Technical Staff at Anthropic”, working on Claude Code.

The lessons and the open question

  • 1. Nathen Harvey (DORA Lead) and Derek DeBellis, “Announcing the 2025 DORA Report: State of AI-Assisted Software Development”, Google Cloud blog, 24 September 2025: nearly 5,000 technology professionals. “Unlike last year, we observe a positive relationship between AI adoption on both software delivery throughput and product performance.” “However, AI adoption does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability.” The 25–35%: Bain & Company, “From Pilots to Payoff: Generative AI in Software Development”, Technology Report 2025, 23 September 2025: “… writing and testing code only accounts for about 25% to 35% of the time from initial idea to product launch.” Its source, in the note to the report’s Figure 1: “Industry experience is based on software-as-a-service developer team surveys”; no sample size is given. The same report: “Teams using AI assistants see 10% to 15% productivity boosts”, and “often the time saved isn’t redirected toward higher-value work.” A figure in the same direction, from a survey with its sample stated: 484 developers at Microsoft, surveyed in June–July 2024, reported the hours of their last week, and coding took about 11% (Kumar, Goel, Zimmermann, Houck, Ashok and Bansal, “Time Warp: The Gap Between Developers’ Ideal vs Actual Workweeks in an AI-Driven Era”, ICSE SEIP 2025, arXiv 2502.15287). Self-reported, one company.
  • 2. David Loker, VP of AI at CodeRabbit, interviewed by Monika Chauhan, “AI Code Quality in 2026: Why Enterprise Leaders Must Prioritize Guardrails Over Speed”, TFiR, 20 February 2026; two sentences of one quoted passage, each shortened at its end. Cursor, “Graphite is joining Cursor”, 19 December 2025: “Graphite has entered into a definitive agreement to be acquired by Cursor.” GitHub, Octoverse 2025, 28 October 2025, for 1 September 2024 to 31 August 2025: 43.2 million pull requests merged a month on average (+23% year on year); comments on issues and pull requests “essentially flat (+0.35%)”. Activity on GitHub, not attributed to AI.
  • 3. Simon Willison, “Not all AI-assisted programming is vibe coding (but vibe coding rocks)”, 19 March 2025. The Linux kernel’s documentation, “AI Coding Assistants”; it also asks contributions to carry an “Assisted-by” tag. The quiz: Shen and Tamkin, “How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills”, Anthropic, 29 January 2026; paper arXiv 2601.20245, a preprint. 52 software engineers, mostly junior, randomly split, learned a new Python library for asynchronous code in a coding task of at most 35 minutes, then took a quiz (about an hour in all): “the AI group averaged 50% on the quiz, compared to 67% in the hand-coding group”. One task, a chat assistant rather than an agent; the authors work at an AI company that sells a coding agent.
  • 4. Margaret-Anne Storey, “How Generative and Agentic AI Shift Concern from Technical Debt to Cognitive Debt”, 9 February 2026. The whole sentence: “Cognitive debt, a term gaining traction recently, instead communicates the notion that the debt compounded from going fast lives in the brains of the developers and affects their lived experiences and abilities to “go fast” or to make changes.” Addy Osmani, “Comprehension Debt – the hidden cost of AI generated code”, 14 March 2026. The +30%: He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner and Vasilescu (Carnegie Mellon), “Speed at the Cost of Quality: How Cursor AI Increases Short-Term Velocity and Long-Term Complexity in Open-Source Projects”, MSR 2026 (arXiv 2511.04427): 806 GitHub repositories that adopted Cursor, against matched projects that did not; a “transient increase in project-level development velocity”, and static-analysis warnings about 30% higher, persistent. Open source only.
  • 5. METR (Parker Whitfill, Cheryl Wu, Joel Becker, Nate Rush), “Many SWE-bench-Passing PRs Would Not Be Merged into Main”, 10 March 2026. The sentence ends “…, even after adjusting for noise in maintainer merge decisions.” 4 maintainers of 3 repositories reviewed 296 agent patches that had passed the benchmark’s grader; the grader’s pass rate was about 24 points above the maintainers’ merge rate, normalized to the rate at which they merged human patches (about 35 points raw). Its caveat: “While models only have one attempt at submitting a solution, human developers iterate with feedback in most cases.” No CI. SWE-Bench Pro: Deng, Da, Pan, He et al., “SWE-Bench Pro: Can AI Agents Solve Long-Horizon Software Engineering Tasks?”, arXiv 2509.16941 (v2, November 2025), Table 3: the stronger of the two models in the table resolved 25.9% of tasks given the problem statement, requirements and interface, and 8.4% given the problem statement only; requirements and interface were removed together. The authors’ explanation (section 6.2): without them “agents are less constrained and can submit more diverse solutions”, and “Since unit tests expect a narrow set of solutions, verifiers are prone to false negatives, resulting in lower pass rates.”
  • 6. StrongDM AI, “Software Factories And The Agentic Moment”, Justin McCarthy, 6 February 2026: its rules, word for word. On the same page, a scenario is “an end-to-end “user story”, often stored outside the codebase (similar to a “holdout” set in model training)”, and “A test, stored in the codebase, can be lazily rewritten to match the code.” It also checks against “behavioral clones of the third-party services our software depends on”. Addy Osmani, “Software Factories, Light and Dark”, 20 July 2026: “A dark software factory is the same move: code ships that no human has read, verified only by other machines.” His rule in full: “A loop can earn itself fully automated status only if the check is cheap, runs at high frequency, and relies on something that can’t be easily faked out.” “A loop needs to be reviewed if a wrong answer is expensive and only a person can catch it.”

What we build

  • The answers describe The Dream Team Life Cycle as DreamTeam runs it. DreamTeam’s repository is private, so its sources are paths in it: the stages and the engineer’s gates in config/workflows.yaml. Each task contract is reviewed before the engineer’s readiness gate by a panel of up to 3 agents (4 for a structural change), the reviewer always among them (.claude/commands/dt-task-init.md L148, L156; tools/panel_selector.py L1095–1102, L1147–1155). A reviewer agent that did not build the change and has no editing tools, and a tester, check every code change (.claude/agents/dt-reviewer.md L8–11: it “never reviews its own work”, runs on the strongest model); the tester writes tests that verify the task contract’s acceptance criteria and maps every criterion to a test (.claude/agents/dt-tester.md L3, L41–45); if two review rounds don’t settle it, the task stops for the engineer (config/workflows.yaml L2364–2375). At the end the task’s closure notes give what was delivered against its criteria, the files changed, the key decisions and the follow-ups, and only the engineer starts the close, after reviewing them (.claude/commands/dt-task-finalize.md L56–61, L109). Nothing in the cycle asks the engineer to read every line.
