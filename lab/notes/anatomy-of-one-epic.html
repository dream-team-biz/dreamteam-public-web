Anatomy of one epic

One real DreamTeam epic, from an engineer's problem to working, checked software

Job cards say what they wait for and why, or the foreman refuses them. An engineer's problem went through a brainstorm, fourteen tasks and independent checks, and came out as working, checked software — at a cost shown task by task.

How the brainstorm planned it: How a brainstorm turned a problem into nine tasks

Tasks started before the work they needed existed, and stopped halfway

DreamTeam ran tasks out of the planned order, and some stopped halfway: a part another task was meant to build did not exist yet.

The board, DreamTeam's list of tasks, was right about every wait it recorded. The failure was omission, not wrong order.

The damage came from waits never written down: 264 of 685 tasks named nothing they waited for — a task may rightly need nothing, but none said so where the board reads — and 47 stated an order only in their own words.

The brainstorm traced the cause and planned the fix as this epic: a larger goal with promised outcomes (acceptance criteria), closed only on evidence.

In the brainstorm · its vision

“in the end I waste time and tokens on something that I don't do”

Only two can be written as “this task must follow that one”.

The engineer's words, a fragment; then its finding.

Every task is on the record: when it ran, where it came from, what it would cost

This picture holds the whole epic. Each of the 14 tasks passed a reviewer agent and a tester before it closed.

One decision up close: the engineer held the switch that turned refusal on. On 23 September a review found it not ready: the text checker, which had to pass first, still failed, and a trial switch broke 24 tests.

It restarted on 5 October; before any change, the checker printed 31 findings, none that blocks. It merged the next day.

Nine tasks planned, fourteen done

The plan the engineer approved had nine tasks in six waves; the first, Dependency record, has its own note: Anatomy of one task.

The work added five: one planned task split in two, and four new ones, all born outside a brainstorm — four on the engineer's instruction, one with his approval. The four new tasks all landed before the switch to refusal. Why each was added.

Each open task now says what it waits for; a new one that doesn't is refused

The fix is a duty: every open task records what it waits for and why, or “none” with a reason. A checker reads each task's notes, criteria and contract, which holds a copy of the record, for orders the record misses.

The checker judges a reason's form only — blank, very short or repeated — never its sense. It flags a “none” when the task's own words state an order; weak reasons on links are reported, not refused — 22 at the first grade.

By backfill, 72 of the 73 open tasks without a record got one (the 73rd had finished): a tool proposed them, and the agent that runs the work applied them under the engineer's rulings, in 17 batches, each merged before the next.

Graded by reviewer agents that built none of it, then closed by the engineer

Fresh reviewer agents, with no part in building it, graded the eight promised outcomes read-only: the grade and the re-grade, each at its own pinned commit.

The first grade failed two. The engineer had one fixed inside the closing task, and the other rewritten as a live example.

A fresh re-grade passed both, on narrow evidence. The engineer then approved the final wording, which names the board as it stood at commit ae9bd1943; the agent that runs the work re-checked it, and no fresh reviewer graded it again.

The one carried over is the problem itself: whether tasks still stall. It can only be graded across a whole later epic, and waits for another, already under way, to finish.

How the prices were counted

At least, at API list prices: DreamTeam's own record of each step's tokens, plus the helper agents it missed, recovered from the session files, at Anthropic's list prices in force when the work ran. At most, our subscription share: a row's part of each account-week it ran in, × $28.85 a seat-week ($125 a month × 12 ÷ 52); an upper bound, since one account was also used outside Claude Code, one week's files had lost messages, and one week was still open.
